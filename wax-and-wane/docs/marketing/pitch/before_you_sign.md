# Before you sign anything

This is a plain-language checklist, not legal advice. A publishing contract is a legal document: have an adult
you trust read it with you, and a lawyer if you possibly can. **If you are under 18, a parent or guardian has
to sign, and usually has to own the store and bank accounts.** Say so early; publishers deal with it often.

## What a publishing deal usually is

The publisher pays for and does some of: marketing, store pages, testing, translation, console versions. In
return they take a share of the money the game earns, for a set number of years, on set platforms.

## Check these eight things

1. **You keep the game.** The contract should say you own the game, its name and its characters (the "IP").
   The publisher gets a licence to sell it, not ownership. Walk away from any deal that takes the IP.
2. **The split, and what comes off first.** "50/50" means little until you know what is taken off before the
   split: store fees, taxes, refunds, and the publisher's own costs ("recoup"). Ask for an example with real
   numbers: "if the game earns $10,000 in a month, how much reaches me?"
3. **Which costs they can recoup, and the cap.** Marketing and porting costs taken out of your share should be
   listed, agreed in advance and capped. No cap means you may never see a payment.
4. **Which platforms and how long.** A deal for consoles only, for five years, is very different from every
   platform forever. Keep any platform they are not going to work on.
5. **What they promise to do.** The contract should list it: which platforms, which languages, by when. "Best
   efforts" with no list is a promise of nothing.
6. **How it ends.** If they do not release it, stop selling it or go out of business, the rights should come
   back to you automatically. Look for words like "reversion" and "termination".
7. **Who holds the store pages.** If the Steam page is under their account, find out how it moves to you when
   the deal ends. Your wishlists and reviews live on that page.
8. **Money to you up front.** Some deals pay an advance or a "minimum guarantee". It is usually taken back out
   of your share later. Nice to have, not a reason to accept a bad split.

## Warning signs

- They ask **you** to pay them. Real publishers pay their own costs. (A porting studio that charges a fee is a
  service, which is fine, but it is not a publisher.)
- Pressure to sign today.
- They will not put a promise in writing.
- They want the IP, or rights "in all media, forever".
- They found you, are very flattering, and you cannot find games they have actually released.

## Before you say yes

- Look up three games they published. Did those get marketing? Are they still on sale? What do the reviews say?
- Ask to talk to a developer they have worked with.
- Sleep on it.
